# Evidence policy

Use this policy when factual or professional-domain claims influence a model.

## Source priority

1. Current official problem statement, attachments, standards, laws, and authoritative datasets.
2. Peer-reviewed papers, authoritative technical reports, and recognized reference works.
3. Preprints with transparent methods, reproducible data, or independently supported conclusions.
4. University, laboratory, professional-society, and manufacturer technical materials.
5. General websites, news, blogs, forums, and search snippets.

Lower tiers may identify leads but must not silently carry a stronger conclusion than they support. A highly relevant lower-tier source can complement, but not impersonate, primary evidence.

## What to extract

For each material source, record:

- stable citation or URL;
- source type and publication status;
- studied system and experimental or analytical setting;
- variable and symbol meanings;
- assumptions and parameter range;
- exact relevant conclusion;
- locator such as section, equation, theorem, table, figure, or page;
- difference between the source setting and the competition problem;
- allowed use: direct transfer, parameter prior, qualitative support, or lead only.

## Claim-strength rules

- A source proves what it actually studied, not every analogous scenario.
- Correlation does not establish a mechanism without additional evidence.
- A numerical parameter is not transferable without checking units, regime, population, material, and measurement conditions.
- A threshold or reversal requires its defining variable, direction, range, and uncertainty.
- An abstract or search snippet is insufficient for a decisive technical claim when the full source is available.
- When reliable sources disagree, present the disagreement and test both plausible regimes when it can change the answer.
- When evidence is missing, write `unresolved`; do not fill the gap with plausibility.

## Research handoff

Return findings in two sections:

1. Background, symbols, assumptions, applicability, and evidence quality.
2. Problem-relevant conclusions, case divisions, special effects, and modeling consequences.
